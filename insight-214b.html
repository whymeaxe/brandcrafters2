What a 214(b) refusal means—and what it does not mean.

A 214(b) refusal can feel final because the interview is short and the written notice is general. Understanding the official rule helps separate facts from speculation.

This article provides general information based on public U.S. Department of State guidance. It is not legal advice and cannot explain the private reasoning in any individual consular decision.

What the official guidance says

INA section 214(b) applies to nonimmigrant visa categories. The U.S. Department of State explains that a refusal under this section means the applicant did not sufficiently demonstrate qualification for the requested nonimmigrant category and/or did not overcome the statutory presumption of immigrant intent where applicable.

The refusal is for that specific application. The consular section cannot continue acting on the closed case, and there is no appeal process for a 214(b) decision.

Can you apply again?

Yes. A future attempt requires a new application and the application fee again. The Department of State advises that an applicant reapplying after a 214(b) refusal should be able to present evidence of significant changes in circumstances or additional information relevant to eligibility.

Reapplication should be treated as a new case—not an opportunity to repeat the same information with better-sounding sentences.

What 214(b) does not automatically tell you

The refusal notice identifies a legal ground, but it usually does not provide a personalised forensic explanation of every concern considered in the interview. An independent consultant also does not have access to consular notes or internal systems.

A responsible review therefore works with observable information:

  • The submitted DS-160 and prior application record
  • The applicant’s recollection of questions and answers
  • Purpose, funding, ties, travel and family facts
  • Differences between the previous and proposed new application
  • Any genuine changes since refusal

Common mistakes after refusal

Reapplying immediately without a case review

A new appointment does not make the facts stronger. If the same risks remain, the applicant may repeat the same result.

Changing answers without a genuine factual reason

Unexplained differences can create credibility questions. Current facts should be accurate, and material changes should have a truthful explanation.

Assuming one document will “solve” the case

Documents support a case. They do not replace eligibility, coherent purpose, accurate disclosure or a credible overall profile.

Memorising a new script

Rehearsed answers can fail under follow-up questions. The applicant should understand the case and respond naturally.

A disciplined reapplication checklist

  1. Obtain the old DS-160 or the most complete record available.
  2. Write the interview sequence immediately and accurately.
  3. Identify what has genuinely changed since the refusal.
  4. Compare old and current facts for discrepancies.
  5. Review purpose, finances, ties, family and travel context together.
  6. Seek legal advice where misrepresentation, overstay, criminal history or statutory inadmissibility may be involved.
  7. Submit only after the new application is truthful, coherent and decision-ready.
